## Whose Resilience?

Technology is partial and, in the case of digital education, it is translational – an unavoidable intermediary between student and learner (Perrotta et al., 2021, p. 108; Macgilchrist et al., 2024, p. 19).

Reliant as it is on technology for all activities, digital education exists within a digital economy that is currently the most valuable economic sector on the globe (Komljenovic, 2021, p. 321). Digital education is potentially highly vulnerable, and these power dynamics must be critically considered. You will read four different definitions of resilience in digital education.

Keep the following ‘utopia as method’ questions in mind as you read the next passages:

- Who or what is **promised**?
- Who or what is **prioritised**?
- Who or what is **deprioritised or neglected** in each case?
- Who or what **benefits** from the status quo?
- What **change** might offer opportunity?

### Resilience as Adaptation

Weller and Anderson (2013, p. 54) define resilience as the continuation of pedagogical functions (pedagogies & people) over technological form (spaces & tools), arguing that ‘a clear distinction’ between the two allows scholarship to survive any circumstance. They prioritise adaptation to preserve scholarly functions:

> _‘Resilience requires adaptation and evolution to new environmental conditions, but retains core identity. In ecosystems this means the species persists, although it may be adapted, and in organisational terms it means the core functions remain’ (p. 55)_

Such a stance becomes diminishingly tenable as platforms become integral infrastructures rather than ancillary services (Komljenovic, 2021, p. 328; Komljenovic et al., 2024, pp. 282–283). Adaptation risks cementing institutional subservience to market forces – this resilience neglects institutional identity and agency.

### Resilience as Individualism

Contemporary resilience discourse may frame it as an individual virtue – an internal capacity to endure changing conditions. Phrases like lifelong learning and job marketability position learners as subjects of resilience, while absolving institutions, governments, and industry of responsibility for governance over their systems. Cretney (2014, p. 633) captures this shift:

> _‘mainstream discourses of resilience have quickly moved from pursuing the boundaries of a system to focussing on individual responsibility and preparedness’_

Individualistic discourse fails to attend to any responsibility for stability or social coherence, and Facer (2016, p. 68) critiques this movement as a fantasy that minimises social complexities, instead marking education an end in and of itself:

> _‘it deracinates education from the divergent, conflicting forces that also contribute to creating meaningful foundations for the growth of a good society’_

There are defensible arguments to be made for individual resilience, in the face of unexpected crisis for example, but in an age of fast-paced disruption and employment uncertainty, is individual resilience as policy the correct priority? Who benefits from a digital education (or society) that is in constant flux: change-makers or changed?

### Resilience as Collectivism

Counter-discourses position institutions as having a duty to produce resilience in learners. Benavot et al. (2024) argue education must enable communities to ‘withstand, respond and adapt to future crises and disruptions’, while Cameron et al. (2024) frame resilience as a policy and practice for institutional or educational continuity through adversity.

Both position resilience as a collective capacity cultivated through pedagogy and governance, aligning with Weller and Anderson’s (2013) emphasis on institutional capacity to absorb and adapt to change.

This collective responsibility raises further questions: which structures enable collective response rather than individual endurance, and do responsibilities align with the power to meaningfully affect change (Cretney, 2014, p. 633)? What approaches might redistribute burden and agency?

### Resilience as Market Alignment

Market alignment ensures social relevance but should not be the sole purpose of education (Lambrechts, 2020, p. 7). Such discourse may also tip concerningly over into markets defining social relevance. When resilience is framed through market alignment, capacity to endure disruption is reconfigured as market readiness: adapting to edtech platforms, adopting market trends, producing graduates for labour needs (Cretney, 2014, p. 633).

Technology corporations benefit from such framing: they profit from cyclical imaginaries of innovation and upskilling, adoption of proprietary platforms, and extract value through data and recurring revenue (Komljenovic, 2022, p. 130).

This resilience prioritises the socio-economic status quo and raises critical questions: What does it mean when market alignment becomes a highly valued resilience? How do benefits shift, whose interests are served, and how might institutions escape captive infrastructures to enact preferred structures of education?
