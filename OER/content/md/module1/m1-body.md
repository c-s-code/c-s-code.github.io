## Note - Good Society

The methodology makes frequent reference to ‘good society’, so it is important to clarify. The ‘good society’ is a hopeful hypothetical model of being for people (subjects) and their contexts (objects) (Levitas, 2013; Bayne, 2024).

It can prospectively look forwards, as will be the case in the architecture mode, but is equally applicable as a retrospective model of what is or was. ‘Good’ here indicates desire for better rather than inherent benevolence, as good for one is not necessarily good for all.

## Utopia as Method - A Futures Methodology

### Archaeology

The archaeological mode involves examining the objects – discourses, policies, imaginaries, infrastructures – that reflect the intended state of digital education and its participants. Through interrogating their explicit account, as well as their ‘gaps and silences’, we uncover the tendencies and priorities of the ‘good society’ in question (Bayne, 2024, p. 510).

Current policies on AI literacy offer a contemporary example of a specific ‘good society’ vision – it forwards an urgency and economic imperative to embed AI literacy as a core life skill, compulsory for successful future employment (Procter, 2026).

### Ontology

Ontology examines presuppositions about the subjects of digital education, which Bayne (2024, p. 512) describes as considering ‘the ways of being that are assumed and enabled’. We model the subjects of a ‘good society’ by examining the kinds of stakeholders (students, educators etc) that are expected to exist: their behaviours, competencies, aspirations, and so on.

Some ways of being will have been explicitly exposed through archaeology, yet more implicit states may also be unearthed. Consider an edtech vendor that markets their AI products as ‘enabling innovation’ – what ontological norm are they forwarding? AI-literacy is one, which drags with it an AI future imaginary. Consider the kind of student or teacher produced by that. Who or what is driving this discourse, and is the goal readily apparent?

### Architecture

Architecture builds upon archaeology and ontology to ask what could be different. To build a ‘good society’ that reflects your own future vision, it requires a ‘suspension of disbelief’ to imagine alternative routes through digital education that are unfazed by the archaeologically-unearthed biases and tendencies (Bayne, 2024, p. 514). Utopia as method is iterative, and so architecture demands a return to archaeology to reflect on the alternative ‘good society’ to uncover new (or repeated) assumptions.

## Why Utopia as Method?

This methodology, as Bayne (2024) has demonstrated, allows us to treat platforms as active participants that influence education through their mediative positioning (Perrotta et al., 2021, p. 108). Such framing ensures the material impact and discursive presence of technology is critically considered.
